# **Simulating the Macroeconomic Dynamics of AI-Enabled Hyperautomation: An Agent-Based Stock-Flow Consistent Framework** 

## **Introduction to the Hyperautomation Paradox** 

The rapid proliferation of artificial intelligence and machine learning technologies has catalyzed a paradigm shift in industrial and cognitive production, a phenomenon increasingly defined as "hyperautomation." Unlike preceding technological revolutions characterized primarily by capital-augmenting or labor-augmenting improvements, modern artificial intelligence possesses the distinct capability to autonomously execute a vast array of complex cognitive and manual tasks, fundamentally displacing human labor from the production process. At the microeconomic level, individual firms are heavily incentivized to adopt these hyperautomation technologies to drastically reduce overhead, minimize variable costs, and maximize theoretical profit margins. However, when this microeconomic optimization is aggregated across a macroeconomy, it precipitates a profound structural crisis: a fatal decoupling of production capacity from aggregate consumption. 

The paradox of hyperautomation lies in the dual economic role of the human worker. In a capitalist economy, the worker is both a factor of production and the primary source of aggregate demand. If an entire economy adopts hyperautomation simultaneously, the resultant wave of technological unemployment severely erodes the purchasing power of the general population. Firms, having achieved unprecedented production rates and theoretical profit margins, will subsequently face collapsed consumer markets. This leads to unsold inventories, collapsing prices, and systemic corporate insolvency. 

To rigorously analyze, visualize, and communicate this highly non-linear, out-of-equilibrium dynamic, traditional Dynamic Stochastic General Equilibrium (DSGE) models are fundamentally inadequate. DSGE models rely on representative agents, perfect foresight, and continuous market clearing, explicitly precluding the study of emergent crises, heterogeneous financial fragility, and systemic coordination failures. Instead, the optimal methodology for exploring the delicate balance between hyperautomated production rates and human consumption rates is the integration of Agent-Based Modeling (ABM) with Stock-Flow Consistent (SFC) macroeconomic accounting. 

This report details the comprehensive theoretical architecture, computational infrastructure, and visualization paradigm required to construct a high-fidelity, highly configurable AB-SFC digital twin of a national economy. The proposed simulation framework enables researchers and policymakers to observe the real-time rollout of hyperautomation intervals, tracking individual agents as they interact in labor, goods, and housing markets, and visualizing the ultimate societal and corporate ramifications when purchasing power is structurally eroded. 

## **Theoretical Foundations of the Simulation** 

To construct a valid economic simulation that accurately reflects the consequences of a systemic technological shock, the behavior of the agents must be strictly grounded in heterodox macroeconomic theory and advanced labor economics. The simulation architecture integrates three foundational pillars: the Task-Based Approach to Automation, Stock-Flow Consistent accounting, and Debt-Deflation mechanics. 

### **The Task-Based Framework of Technological Displacement** 

The primary mechanism by which hyperautomation affects the economy in this simulation is derived from the task-based framework formalized by Acemoglu and Restrepo. Standard macroeconomic production functions typically assume that technology strictly augments labor or capital (e.g., standard Cobb-Douglas formulations). In contrast, the task-based framework treats specific tasks as the fundamental unit of production. Output Y is generated by combining a continuum of tasks z \in [N-1, N]. 

Each task can be performed either by human labor l(z) or by capital, which in this context represents AI, denoted as k(z). The production of task z is mathematically defined as y(z) = A_L \gamma_L(z) l(z) + A_K \gamma_K(z) k(z), where A_L and A_K represent general factor-augmenting technologies, and \gamma_L(z) and \gamma_K(z) denote the task-specific productivity of labor and capital, respectively. 

In the simulation, hyperautomation is modeled as a discrete, configurable shock to an exogenous parameter I, which represents the threshold of automated tasks. As hyperautomation rolls out in specified intervals, I increases, shifting tasks previously performed by humans into the domain of AI. This triggers three distinct economic forces within the simulation environment. First, the displacement effect occurs when capital directly replaces labor in a specific set of tasks, explicitly reducing the labor share of value added and creating immediate technological unemployment. Second, the productivity effect emerges because AI performs these tasks at a fraction of the cost, thereby decreasing the firm's overall cost of production. This theoretically allows firms to lower prices, which can increase real income for consumers and potentially expand demand for non-automated tasks. Third, the reinstatement effect represents the creation of entirely new, human-centric tasks, effectively expanding the parameter N. 

The critical insight driving the simulation's crisis scenario is that "so-so" automation—technologies that displace workers but yield only marginal aggregate productivity gains—fails to generate a sufficient productivity effect to absorb displaced workers. Furthermore, if the rollout of hyperautomation outpaces the reinstatement effect, the net displacement dominates, permanently eroding the aggregate wage bill. 

### **Stock-Flow Consistent (SFC) Macroeconomics** 

To ensure that the erosion of purchasing power correctly translates into corporate losses and banking crises, the simulation must adhere strictly to the principles of Stock-Flow Consistent (SFC) modeling. In an SFC model, every financial flow is accounted for, adhering to the principle that "everything comes from somewhere and goes somewhere". 

Every agent in the simulation, including households, firms, banks, and the central government, possesses a rigorous balance sheet. Any transaction between agents requires quadruple-entry bookkeeping. For example, when a firm pays a worker, the firm's deposit account decreases, representing a use of funds, while the household's deposit account increases, representing a source of funds. Simultaneously, the transaction is recorded in both agents' income statements. 

The accounting architecture is governed by a Balance Sheet Matrix and a Transaction Flow Matrix. The Balance Sheet Matrix ensures that all assets and liabilities sum to zero across the economy, preventing arbitrary wealth creation. To demonstrate how funds circulate and where the systemic bottlenecks occur during a hyperautomation shock, the Transaction Flow Matrix tracks all current and capital accounts.

To maintain consumption, households must draw down existing deposits or increase consumer debt. Once credit limits are exhausted, consumption mathematically must fall, directly reducing firm revenues in the Transaction Flow Matrix and triggering a macroeconomic contraction. 

### **Fisherian Debt-Deflation Spirals** 

The ultimate macro-level consequence of hyperautomation in this constrained economy is modeled via the Debt-Deflation theory, originally posited by Irving Fisher and later expanded in complex systems economics. This theory explains the catastrophic feedback loop that occurs when highly leveraged entities face a sudden collapse in revenue. 

When firms automate heavily, they take on significant debt to finance the acquisition of AI capital. If the aggregated displacement of workers leads to a collapse in consumption, these firms find themselves with massive inventories of unsold goods. To generate liquidity to service their debts, firms are forced into distress selling, aggressively slashing the prices of their goods. However, because their liabilities are fixed in nominal terms, the deflation of goods prices actually increases the real burden of their debt. The real interest rate spikes, leading to mass corporate defaults. These defaults generate non-performing loans on bank balance sheets, eroding bank equity and forcing them to restrict further credit, which spirals the economy into a deep depression. This is the exact mechanism the simulation seeks to visualize: a company-level drive for hyper-efficiency creating a systemic macro-level collapse due to the destruction of the consumer base. 

## **Agent-Level Specifications and Market Protocols** 

To simulate a whole country with a fixed population where users can observe individual effects such as homes, rent, and loans, the model relies on a decentralized, bottom-up architecture. Aggregate statistics emerge organically from localized microeconomic interactions. The core of the system is a variant of the "Dystopian Schumpeter meeting Keynes" (DSK) model, expanded to include detailed household financial constraints and a robust housing market. 

### **Household Agents, Labor Matching, and Consumption** 

The simulation initializes a fixed population of heterogeneous household agents representing the citizenry. Each household possesses a distinct array of state variables that define its economic standing and behavioral parameters. 

|Household State Variable|Description and Function in Simulation|
|---|---|
|**Employment Status**|Boolean value indicating whether the agent is<br>employed. Dictatesprimaryincome streams.|
|**Skill Level**|Numeric value defining the agent's productivity<br>and vulnerability to specific automation<br>intervals.|

|**Outstanding Debt**|Nominal value of accumulated consumer loans<br>and mortgageprincipal.|
|**Propensity to Consume**|Behavioral parameter dictating the percentage<br>of disposable income spent on consumption<br>goods.|



At each tick of the simulation, representing one economic month, households execute a sequence of decisions. Unemployed households search the job market, evaluating posted vacancies from firms. This matching process is governed by a Diamond-Mortensen-Pissarides (DMP) search friction protocol, meaning agents have limited visibility of the entire market and cannot instantly find optimal matches. 

Following the labor matching phase, households determine their nominal consumption budget. This budget is based on a Keynesian propensity to consume out of current disposable income and accumulated wealth. If a household becomes unemployed due to hyperautomation, its consumption drops to a bare subsistence level, funded entirely by drawing down savings or taking on debt. Finally, households must service their housing costs, paying rent to landlord agents or servicing their mortgage to the banking sector. 

### **Firm Agents, Pricing Heuristics, and The Firing Mechanism** 

The simulated economy features firm agents divided into two categories: consumption goods producers (C-Firms) and capital/AI goods producers (K-Firms). Firm behavior is not governed by perfect rationality, but rather by bounded rationality and heuristic decision-making protocols. 

|Firm State Variable|Description and Function in Simulation|
|---|---|
|**Task Efficiency Level**|Configurable parameter determining how much<br>output a single unit of labor or AI canproduce.|
|**Automation Ratio**|The percentage of the firm's production tasks<br>currently executed by AI rather than human<br>labor.|
|**Inventory Stock**|Accumulated unsold goods resulting from a<br>mismatch between production rates and<br>aggregate consumption.|

|**Price Markup**|Heuristic variable adjusted based on recent<br>salesperformance and inventoryaccumulation.|



Firms forecast demand based on previous sales cycles, determining their required output and setting a target markup over their unit costs. The critical dynamic occurs during the hyperautomation rollout. Based on the configurable global rollout parameter, firms evaluate the cost of human labor versus the cost of an AI capital upgrade. When the hyperautomation interval triggers, firms take out bank loans to purchase task-automation upgrades from the K-Firms. 

Upon automating tasks, the firm calculates its new labor requirement. Because task efficiency has dramatically increased, the firm can produce the same or greater output with a fraction of the workforce. Surplus workers are immediately fired to optimize the firm's cost structure. If the aggregate displacement causes overall demand to fall, firms accumulate inventory. A high inventory-to-sales ratio forces the firm to heuristically reduce its price markup, initiating the deflationary cycle. If a firm's cash flow ultimately cannot cover its debt servicing, it defaults, simultaneously firing all remaining workers and generating a sharp shock to the labor market. 

### **The Housing and Credit Markets** 

To fulfill the requirement of observing individual living conditions, rent, and loans, the simulation incorporates a high-fidelity housing market model. Homes are explicitly modeled as spatial assets within the simulation environment. 

When a household seeks to purchase a home, it applies for a mortgage from the bank agent. The bank evaluates the household based on strict Loan-to-Value (LTV) and Debt-to-Income (DTI) regulatory limits. If a household's income falls due to automation and its savings are depleted, it inevitably defaults on its mortgage. This triggers a strict foreclosure protocol where the bank seizes the property and attempts to liquidate it on the real estate market. 

The financial sector acts as the primary transmission mechanism for systemic contagion. Banks accept deposits from households and extend credit to firms for AI upgrades and to households for mortgages. Banks are heavily constrained by Capital Adequacy Ratios (CAR). As firms and households default simultaneously, the bank's equity is rapidly wiped out by non-performing loans. To survive, the bank aggressively rations credit, refusing to roll over the short-term debt of surviving firms, further suffocating the real economy and accelerating the foreclosure cascade.

To ensure the simulation serves as an interactive educational and research tool, it requires a 

highly configurable global state controller. The user must be able to modulate the exact speed, severity, and intervals of the technological shock to observe different macroeconomic trajectories.

The user defines the hyperautomation rollout plan as a sequential step function. ### **Time Speed and Current Date Synchronization** 

In ABM architectures, time is processed in discrete computational intervals known as "ticks" or "steps". The model maps one tick to one economic month. A standard simulation run spans 600 ticks, equating to 50 years, to capture both the short-term boom and the long-term debt-deflation bust. 

To provide an optimal user experience, the interface includes a "Time Speed" parameter, decoupled from the underlying computational tick rate. This allows the user to scrub from the current real-world date forward, observing the slow accumulation of wealth and the sudden cascades of insolvency in real-time, or accelerated time, depending on the analytical requirement. 

## **Computational Backend and Simulation Architecture** 

Simulating a whole country with thousands to millions of distinct interacting agents, while adhering strictly to double-entry SFC accounting, requires a robust, highly optimized computational backend. The simulation relies on advanced software engineering paradigms to handle this unprecedented scale. 

### **Python and the Mesa Framework** 

For a small to medium-scale economy representing tens of thousands of agents, the Python-based Mesa framework serves as the architectural foundation for the ABM development. Mesa provides a highly modular, object-oriented architecture that is ideal for defining the complex behaviors of economic agents. 

In Mesa, the simulation is governed by a core Model class that initializes the spatial grid, network topologies, and the agent population. Time advancement is handled by specialized Scheduler classes. Because the sequence of agent actions profoundly impacts macroeconomic outcomes, Mesa provides advanced scheduling regimes. For instance, a RandomActivation scheduler shuffles the order of agents each tick, preventing path-dependent artifacts where the first firm to act always captures the market. Alternatively, a SimultaneousActivation scheduler records all agent decisions in a staged buffer, resolving all market clearing, such as the DMP labor matching and goods purchasing, simultaneously at the end of the tick. 

Mesa's DataCollector class seamlessly aggregates micro-level agent states, such as individual bank balances and employment status, into macro-level time-series data like GDP, Gini coefficients, and aggregate inflation at every tick, storing them in memory for real-time visualization. 

### **FLAME GPU and Massive Scale Processing** 

While Python is excellent for prototyping and smaller economies, simulating a whole country with a fixed population in the millions encounters insurmountable bottlenecks due to Python's Global Interpreter Lock (GIL) and single-threaded execution model. For massive-scale demographic and economic digital twins, the architecture transitions to the FLAME GPU 2 (Flexible Large-scale Agent Modelling Environment) framework. 

FLAME GPU leverages C++ and NVIDIA's CUDA architecture to execute ABMs directly on the graphics processing unit. Agents are modeled as communicating finite-state machines. During a tick, millions of agents can simultaneously evaluate their SFC budget constraints, search the labor market, and update their state variables in massive parallel across thousands of GPU cores. This allows for unprecedented scaling without abstracting individuals into representative cohorts, preserving the absolute heterogeneity required to observe exactly how localized foreclosures trigger neighborhood-specific housing crashes. 

## **WebGL Visualization Paradigm: Bridging Micro and Macro** 

The user requirements explicitly request a visualization that simulates an economy where the observer can still see each individual, including their homes, rent, and spatial interactions. Standard ABM visualizations, such as basic Matplotlib grids, are entirely insufficient for this level of immersion and data density. To achieve an optimal, high-performance visualization, the architecture utilizes a custom WebGL frontend built on the Three.js library, deeply integrated with the simulation backend. 

### **GPU Instancing for Massive Entity Rendering** 

Rendering tens of thousands of individual houses, citizens, and corporate headquarters in a 3D space traditionally cripples browser performance due to immense CPU overhead. Every distinct object typically requires a separate draw call sent from the CPU to the GPU; drawing 10,000 distinct houses would result in 10,000 draw calls per frame, dropping the frame rate to unplayable levels. 

The optimal rendering solution is WebGL GPU Instancing, implemented in Three.js via the InstancedMesh class. Instancing allows the application to send the geometric data of a house or a human to the GPU exactly once. The rendering engine then issues a single draw call to render thousands of copies of that geometry. 

To individualize these instances—showing exactly which houses are foreclosed, which citizens are unemployed, and where they are moving—the application utilizes a transformation matrix array. Each instance has a dedicated transformation matrix encoding its unique position, rotation, and scale, alongside a dedicated color attribute. The visual grammar is strictly defined to communicate economic health: a green house indicates a homeowner is employed and servicing their mortgage; a red house indicates the homeowner is unemployed, savings are depleted, and they are facing default; a grey house indicates a foreclosed property sitting vacant on the bank's balance sheet. 

By utilizing raycasting techniques, the user can click on any individual instance within the 3D space to pull up a dynamic UI overlay. This overlay displays that specific agent's localized balance sheet, revealing their current loan balance, rent obligations, and employment status, fulfilling the requirement for granular, individual-level visibility. 

### **Asynchronous State Synchronization** 

To prevent the heavy computational load of the macroeconomic simulation from blocking the main thread of the browser, which would cause the Three.js UI to stutter or freeze, the architecture employs HTML5 Web Workers and SharedArrayBuffer technologies. The simulation loop runs entirely inside an isolated Web Worker. The Web Worker and the main UI thread share memory directly. At the end of every economic tick, the Worker writes the updated positions, colors, and financial states of the agents directly into a tightly packed Float32Array residing in the shared memory space. The main Three.js render loop simply reads this buffer and updates the instance matrix flags. Furthermore, the WebGLSync API is utilized to guarantee that the GPU rendering pipeline is perfectly synchronized with the simulation's state updates, ensuring smooth visualization of time-speed configurations without visual tearing. 

## **Macroeconomic Outcomes and Societal Impact Analysis** 

When the simulation is executed, the visual and statistical outputs reveal the profound, non-linear dynamics of hyperautomation. The AB-SFC framework explicitly demonstrates that optimizing for individual task efficiency does not linearly scale to macroeconomic prosperity when aggregate demand is fundamentally tied to human labor. 

### **The Corporate Profit Squeeze and the Failure of the Jevons Paradox** 

During the initial stages of the rollout, observers note a sharp divergence between productivity and the wage share, closely mirroring the theoretical predictions regarding the displacement effect. Firms aggressively substitute capital for labor. Initially, firm profitability spikes dramatically as overhead costs vanish. 

A common counter-argument to the threat of technological unemployment is the Jevons Paradox, also known as the rebound effect. The premise is that as production becomes vastly more efficient, prices fall, theoretically stimulating a massive increase in consumer demand that creates new jobs to handle the increased volume. However, the simulation reveals the strict mathematical limitations of this paradox in a hyperautomated state. 

For the Jevons Paradox to save employment, demand for the produced goods must be highly elastic, meaning there is an infinite desire to consume more as prices fall. But human consumption has physical and psychological limits. As the simulation progresses, the marginal propensity to consume naturally diminishes. A household does not need a thousand smartphones, regardless of how cheap hyperautomation makes them. Consequently, the rebound effect fails to generate enough new production volume to require re-hiring the displaced workforce, leading directly to structural unemployment. 

### **Visualizing the Societal Collapse** 

As the simulation crosses into the later intervals, the erosion of purchasing power becomes visually apparent across the 3D landscape. At the micro-level, the visualization shows swaths of citizens turning red as they lose employment. Without wages, their internal SFC accounting drains their deposit balances. To maintain subsistence, they max out consumer loans. Once rejected by banks due to shattered credit scores, they default on their mortgages, and neighborhoods slowly flip to grey as foreclosures cascade. 

At the meso-level, the banking sector's balance sheet deteriorates. Non-performing loans erode bank equity, causing banks to react by tightening credit and refusing to roll over the short-term debt of the firms. Finally, at the macro-level, firms face a total collapse in sales despite operating hyper-efficient AI factories with near-zero marginal labor costs. Their inventories skyrocket. To generate cash to pay down their massive AI-capital acquisition debts, they drastically slash prices. 

This visualizes the Fisherian trap perfectly. The general price level deflates, but the nominal debt of the firms remains static, causing the real value of their debt to increase exponentially just as their revenue goes to zero. The user watches on the UI dashboard as the aggregate GDP plummets and cascading bankruptcies wipe out the corporate sector. The simulation vividly proves that an economy entirely composed of highly efficient producers and zero 

##### consumers is mathematically insolvent. 

### **Policy Sandboxing: Universal Basic Income (UBI)** 

The simulation's ultimate utility is acting as a sandbox to find the equilibrium between production rates and consumption rates. To prevent the debt-deflation spiral and the resulting societal collapse, the user can interactively trigger a Universal Basic Income (UBI) policy. By initiating a UBI funded by corporate automation taxes, the Stock-Flow Consistent architecture automatically re-routes the hyper-profits of the highly automated firms directly into the deposit accounts of the households. The visual dashboard instantly reflects this stabilization. Households return to a solvent state, consumer demand re-establishes a baseline floor, and firms begin clearing their accumulated inventories. However, the simulation also highlights the delicate balance required in policy design. If the UBI is set too high relative to the actual physical production capacity of the AI, the sudden influx of nominal liquidity against a constrained supply of goods triggers severe inflation, demonstrating that finding the exact balance between production and consumption requires meticulous calibration.
